What the Research Says About Therapeutic Massage

Research on massage therapy is large but mixed. Many studies are small, results often depend on what massage is compared with, and many follow people for only a few weeks. The summaries below describe what the studies reported. They are general information, not medical advice or a promise of any result, and people respond differently.

Reviews, and What They Say About Limits

A 2024 review that mapped 17 systematic reviews of massage for pain found that none of its conclusions was rated high-certainty. Seven were rated moderate-certainty, all favoring massage for pain, and conclusions that massage was better than other active approaches were rare.[1] An earlier evidence map of 49 systematic reviews found that the high-quality reviews reached conclusions of low strength of evidence, because few large, rigorous studies had been done.[11]

A 2016 meta-analysis of 67 randomized trials found lower pain scores after massage than after sham massage, no massage or other approaches. The authors made a strong recommendation only for the comparison with no massage and a weak one for the others.[2]

A 2015 review of 26 randomized trials with 2,565 people with muscle and joint problems found low-to-moderate-level evidence that massage reduced pain in the short term compared with no intervention in some groups, such as people with shoulder pain, but not in people with neck or low-back pain. Function improved in the short term for people with low-back and shoulder pain. Compared with another active approach, no clear benefit was seen.[12]

The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health notes that the risk of harm from massage appears to be low, although rare serious side effects such as a blood clot, nerve injury or bone fracture have been reported, and it advises against using massage to put off seeing a health care provider about a medical problem.[9] A 2003 review of published case reports concluded that massage is not entirely risk free but that serious adverse events are probably true rarities, and most of those reported followed unusual techniques or massage by untrained people rather than by massage therapists.[13]

Neck, Shoulders and Back

A 2024 Cochrane review of 33 studies with 1,994 people with neck pain found that, compared with a placebo massage, massage probably makes little or no difference to pain, function or quality of life at up to 12 weeks (low-certainty evidence). A subgroup analysis favored higher doses of eight or more sessions of at least 30 minutes over four weeks, and adverse events were too poorly reported to judge.[10]

A 2015 Cochrane review of 25 trials with 3,096 people with low-back pain found that massage was better than no intervention for pain in the short term but not over the long term, and that the quality of the evidence was low to very low. The authors said they had “very little confidence” in the results. No serious side effects were reported; the most common minor one was more pain, in 1.5 to 25 percent of people.[14]

A 2014 review of 20 trials of soft-tissue massage for shoulder pain found low-quality evidence that massage improved shoulder movement, pain and function compared with no intervention in the short term, right after the sessions ended.[15] In a trial of 80 adults with shoulder pain who were all doing exercises, adding four weeks of massage gave no extra benefit for pain, disability or movement, and a small difference in pain at 12 weeks favored exercise alone.[16]

In a crossover study of 17 healthy adults, muscle activity in the upper-shoulder muscles fell by 19 percent after a short, moderate-pressure massage of the shoulders and neck, compared with 1 percent after sitting quietly. The study measured muscle activity, not pain.[17]

In a pilot study of 45 cardiac sonographers with work-related muscle discomfort, weekly 30-minute massages for 10 weeks, with or without stretching, were followed by some improvement in discomfort scores compared with no intervention. The authors said larger trials are needed.[18]

Exercise, Flexibility and Recovery

A 2020 review of 29 randomized studies with 1,012 people found no evidence that massage improved strength, jumping, sprinting, endurance or fatigue, but it found small improvements in flexibility and in delayed-onset muscle soreness.[3]

A 2016 review of 22 trials of massage after exercise concluded that the effects on performance recovery are rather small and partly unclear. Shorter massage (5 to 12 minutes) tended to show larger effects than longer massage, and the authors questioned whether the limited effects justify using massage as a recovery method in competitive athletes.[19]

In 52 healthy volunteers whose grip had been tired by exercise, five minutes of forearm and hand massage was followed by better grip performance than five minutes of rest or of passive joint movement. The study was small and measured only the minutes right afterward.[20]

In 10 recreationally active women, 30 seconds of massage at the back of the thigh was followed by about a 7 percent larger hip-bend range than no massage, with no change in muscle tension or muscle activity.[21]

Stress, Relaxation and Well-Being

A 2004 meta-analysis of 37 randomized studies found that a single massage was followed by lower state anxiety, blood pressure and heart rate, but not by lower negative mood, lower cortisol or less immediate pain.[22] A 2011 review of massage and the stress hormone cortisol found that the effect was generally very small and in most analyses not statistically distinguishable from zero.[23]

In 60 healthy women, heart-rate variability and feelings of relaxation rose, and feelings of stress fell, in two massage groups and in a resting group; the two massage groups showed higher heart-rate variability than the resting group.[4] In 14 healthy men, a 20-minute massage and a 20-minute reading period were each followed by a rise in the hormone oxytocin, which led the researchers to suggest that touch is not needed for that rise.[24]

In 118 healthy adults who first took a stressful laboratory test, a single massage of about an hour was not followed by significantly better well-being scores or cortisol levels than a sham massage, although most people who had the real massage described it as relaxing (82 to 100 percent, compared with 42 percent after the sham).[25]

In a pilot study of 54 adults aged 60 and over, twice-weekly massage for four weeks was followed by better scores for well-being and perceived stress than guided relaxation.[5]

In a trial of 122 adults at an integrative-therapy clinic in Brazil, people had eight massage sessions followed by rest, massage followed by Reiki, or no intervention. Stress fell more in the massage-plus-Reiki group than in the no-intervention group, and the authors called for a placebo group in later trials.[7] The same trial’s report on stress and quality of life (101 people analyzed) found that both massage groups did better than no intervention.[8]

In 38 family caregivers at a rehabilitation hospital who had either one or three massages a week for two weeks, psychological symptom scores were lower afterward in both groups. Because everyone had massage, the study cannot show how much of the change was due to the massage itself.[6]
