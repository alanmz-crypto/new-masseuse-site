# Thai Solitude — Expanded Peer-Reviewed Medical Citation Bundle

**Date:** 2026-10-04  
**Purpose:** Agent-ready source set for medical/health claims on the Thai Solitude website.

## Evidence rule

The agent may search beyond PubMed, but a source may be used for a website citation only when it is:

- a peer-reviewed scientific paper;
- a systematic review, meta-analysis, randomized controlled trial, or other clearly identified empirical scientific study;
- directly relevant to the exact intervention and outcome being claimed;
- used with wording no stronger than the study evidence.

**Do not use** Mayo Clinic, NCCIH, WebMD, government health pages, Wikipedia, blogs, AI summaries, commercial wellness pages, or marketing copy as the scientific citation itself.

Publisher pages, institutional repositories, Google Scholar, Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect, Wiley, SAGE, BMJ, and journal sites may be used to locate or download the peer-reviewed paper.

# 1. A1 — Therapeutic Massage: Pain / Function / Recovery / Relaxation / Well-Being

## Current website claim

> “Research suggests that massage therapy may help reduce pain, improve mobility, support recovery from physical activity, and promote relaxation. Regular massage has also been associated with improvements in overall well-being, making it a valuable part of a long-term self-care and recovery routine.”

## Strict assessment

**The original sentence is not fully supported by one source.**

It combines distinct evidence areas:

- pain
- mobility/function
- sports/exercise recovery
- relaxation/stress
- quality of life/well-being
- long-term self-care

These should not be presented as though one systematic review proves all of them.

---

## 1A. Pain and function — strong general evidence

### Crawford et al., 2016

**The Impact of Massage Therapy on Function in Pain Populations—A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials: Part I, Patients Experiencing Pain in the General Population**

Peer-reviewed systematic review/meta-analysis.

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27165971/
- PMC full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4925170/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/pm/pnw099
- PMID: 27165971
- PMCID: PMC4925170

### Evidence relevant to website wording

The review included 67 studies and found massage therapy beneficial for pain compared with sham, no treatment, and active comparators. It also found benefits for anxiety and health-related quality of life compared with active comparators.

### What it supports

- “massage therapy may reduce pain”
- “massage therapy has been associated with some improvements in function-related outcomes”
- appropriately qualified quality-of-life / anxiety claims

### What it does not support by itself

- exercise recovery specifically
- all forms of “mobility”
- long-term self-care behavior
- a general statement that massage creates lasting wellness

---

## 1B. Musculoskeletal disorders — short-term pain and function

### Bervoets et al., 2015

**Massage therapy has short-term benefits for people with common musculoskeletal disorders compared to no treatment: a systematic review**

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26093806/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jphys.2015.05.018
- PMID: 26093806

### Evidence

26 randomized trials involving 2,565 participants were reviewed.

The authors found low-to-moderate evidence that massage reduced pain in the short term compared with no treatment in shoulder pain and knee osteoarthritis, but not low-back or neck pain. Low-to-moderate evidence also supported short-term functional improvement in low-back pain, knee arthritis, and shoulder pain.

### Important wording limitation

Do **not** convert this into:

> “Massage improves pain and function in all musculoskeletal problems.”

It does not.

# 2. A1 — Exercise recovery / flexibility / soreness

## Davis et al., 2020

**Effect of sports massage on performance and recovery: a systematic review and meta-analysis**

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32426160/
- PMC full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7228568/
- BMJ journal page: https://bmjopensem.bmj.com/content/6/1/e000614
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjsem-2019-000614
- PMID: 32426160
- PMCID: PMC7228568

### Evidence

29 randomized studies; 1,012 participants.

The meta-analysis found:

- no evidence of improvement in strength;
- no evidence of improvement in jumping;
- no evidence of improvement in sprinting;
- no evidence of improvement in endurance;
- no evidence of improvement in fatigue;
- **small statistically significant improvement in flexibility**;
- **small statistically significant improvement in DOMS**.

### Exact limitation

The intervention was **sports massage**.

Do not silently broaden it to “all therapeutic massage” or “deep tissue massage.”

### Safe wording

> “Research in exercise settings has found small improvements in flexibility and delayed-onset muscle soreness following massage.”

# 3. A1 — Additional exercise-recovery evidence

## Dupuy et al., 2018

**An Evidence-Based Approach for Choosing Post-exercise Recovery Techniques to Reduce Markers of Muscle Damage, Soreness, Fatigue, and Inflammation: A Systematic Review With Meta-Analysis**

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29755363/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2017-0618
- PMID: 29755363

### Evidence

The review included 99 studies of post-exercise recovery techniques.

Massage reduced DOMS, and massage was among the interventions with the largest observed benefit for DOMS/fatigue outcomes.

### Caution

This supports **post-exercise recovery/soreness**, not a general claim about chronic pain, therapeutic massage, or long-term wellness.

# 4. A1 — Relaxation / stress

## Zhang et al., 2021

**Physical relaxation for occupational stress in healthcare workers: a systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials**

Peer-reviewed review in the **Journal of Occupational Health**. Publisher page is available directly from Wiley.

- Wiley full text: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/1348-9585.12243
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1002/1348-9585.12243
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34235817/
- PMC full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8263904/
- PMID: 34235817
- PMCID: PMC8263904

### Evidence

15 randomized trials; 688 healthcare workers.

In network meta-analysis, **massage therapy alone** was more effective than control for occupational stress:

- SMD = -0.43
- 95% CI -0.72 to -0.14

### Safe wording

> “Massage therapy has been associated with reduced stress in some studied populations.”

Or, more specifically:

> “A systematic review of randomized trials found massage therapy reduced occupational stress in healthcare workers.”

### Important limitation

The population is **healthcare workers**, not the general public.

Do not turn this into:

> “Massage reduces stress for everyone.”

# 5. A1 — Psychological well-being from repeated massage

## Williams et al., 2019

**Therapeutic massage to enhance family caregivers' well-being in a rehabilitation hospital**

Peer-reviewed original research in **Complementary Therapies in Clinical Practice**.

- ScienceDirect / publisher page: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1744388118307175
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctcp.2019.03.020
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31003683/
- PMID: 31003683

### Design

38 family caregivers were randomized to:

- one massage per week, or
- three massages per week

for two weeks.

### Findings

Post-program symptom scores decreased in both groups, with statistically significant changes. The study reported high program acceptability and perceived benefits.

### What it supports

This provides direct evidence that **repeated massage sessions can be associated with improvements in psychological well-being in a defined population**.

### What it does NOT support

It does not establish a general long-term wellness benefit for the general public.

# 6. A1 — Self-management / ongoing practice

## Nemati et al., 2024

**Massage Therapy as a Self-Management Strategy for Musculoskeletal Pain and Chronic Conditions: A Systematic Review of Feasibility and Scope**

Peer-reviewed systematic review in the **Journal of Integrative and Complementary Medicine**.

- SAGE publisher page: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1089/jicm.2023.0271
- Institutional repository copy: https://scholarworks.indianapolis.iu.edu/items/5ef12d33-d44a-4c89-9b17-ce42928e7dec
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1089/jicm.2023.0271
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37878283/
- PMID: 37878283

### Evidence

17 studies; 770 participants with musculoskeletal pain or chronic conditions.

The studies involved self-administered massage. Prescribed duration ranged from one session to 8–12 weeks, with four weeks being the most common.

Some studies found significant improvements in outcomes including:

- chronic neck/back pain;
- stress/anxiety;
- fatigue;
- sleep quality;
- health-related quality of life.

### Critical limitation for Thai Solitude

This evidence concerns **massage therapy as self-management / self-administered massage**.

It does **not** directly prove:

> “Regular therapist-provided massage is a valuable long-term self-care routine.”

### What it can legitimately support

A narrower statement such as:

> “Research has also explored massage as part of ongoing self-management for musculoskeletal pain and chronic conditions.”

That is substantially more defensible.

# 7. A1 — Current evidence-quality context

## Mak et al., 2024

**Use of Massage Therapy for Pain, 2018-2023: A Systematic Review**

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39008297/
- PMC full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11250267/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.22259
- PMID: 39008297
- PMCID: PMC11250267

### Why it should anchor website wording

The evidence map identified 129 systematic reviews of massage therapy for painful adult health conditions.

The authors reported that no conclusions from reviews that formally rated certainty were rated as high-certainty; the moderate-certainty conclusions concerned beneficial associations with pain.

### Implementation consequence

This makes highly confident wording inappropriate.

Prefer:

- “may”
- “has been associated with”
- “research suggests”
- “in some conditions”
- “evidence is mixed/varied”

Avoid:

- “proven”
- “guarantees”
- “effective for most people”
- “works for many conditions”
- “lasting result”
- “long-term wellness” as though directly established

# 8. A4 — Deep tissue pain / mobility / quality of life

## Bingölbali et al., 2024

**The effectiveness of deep tissue massage on pain, trigger point, disability, range of motion and quality of life in individuals with myofascial pain syndrome**

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36645811/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/08990220.2023.2165054
- PMID: 36645811

### Direct evidence match

- intervention: deep tissue massage
- population: people with myofascial pain syndrome
- design: randomized controlled trial
- sample: 80 participants
- outcomes: pain, disability, neck range of motion, quality of life

### Stronger website wording

> “In people with myofascial pain syndrome, a randomized trial found that deep tissue massage added to conventional rehabilitation improved pain, disability, quality of life, and several measures of neck range of motion.”

Do not generalize this to every person with musculoskeletal discomfort.

# 9. A4 — Deep tissue neck pain

## Skillgate et al. / STONE trial, 2020

**Effectiveness of deep tissue massage therapy, and supervised strengthening and stretching exercises for subacute or persistent disabling neck pain. The Stockholm Neck (STONE) randomized controlled trial**

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31655314/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.msksp.2019.102070
- PMID: 31655314

### Evidence

Large randomized controlled trial involving 619 adults with subacute or persistent disabling neck pain.

Short-term pain outcomes favored the massage/exercise interventions in some comparisons.

### Critical limitation

At 12 months, there was no clear long-term superiority for pain intensity, and pain-related disability differences were not consistently favorable.

### Website implication

This supports **short-term, condition-specific** language, not “lasting relief” or general effectiveness.

# 10. A5 — Deep tissue flexibility / recovery / functional movement

## Current claim

> “Studies have also linked deep tissue massage with improvements in flexibility, muscle recovery, and functional movement, though outcomes vary from person to person.”

### Strict verdict

**Do not cite this sentence as written.**

The best systematic-review evidence for flexibility and DOMS is on massage in **sports/exercise settings**, not specifically deep tissue massage.

Use Davis et al. (2020) above.

### Recommended rewrite

> “Systematic review evidence in exercise settings has found small improvements in flexibility and delayed-onset muscle soreness following massage, although results vary.”

This preserves the scientifically defensible portion and removes the unsupported deep-tissue-specific attribution.

# 11. A6 — Deep tissue “effective option”

## Koren & Kalichman, 2018

**Deep tissue massage: What are we talking about?**

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29861215/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbmt.2017.05.006
- PMID: 29861215

### Important finding

The review notes that:

- there is no universally accepted definition of deep tissue massage;
- techniques and protocols vary substantially;
- the heterogeneous evidence makes firm conclusions difficult;
- favorable outcomes may occur in pain populations and in people with decreased range of motion.

### Strict disposition of A6

Current wording:

> “Current research suggests deep tissue massage can be an effective option for many types of muscular discomfort and movement restrictions…”

**FAIL.**

### Recommended rewrite

> “Evidence suggests that deep tissue massage may improve pain and some movement-related outcomes in certain musculoskeletal conditions, but findings vary and the evidence is not uniform.”

# 12. What is now supportable for the weaker parts

## “Promote relaxation”

**Supported with qualification.**

Best source:
Zhang et al. 2021, Journal of Occupational Health.

Safe wording:

> “Massage therapy may help reduce stress in some populations.”

Do not make it universal.

---

## “Improve overall well-being”

**Supported only when the population/outcome is specified or the wording is softened.**

Good sources:

- Crawford et al. 2016 — health-related quality of life
- Williams et al. 2019 — psychological well-being in family caregivers
- Nemati et al. 2024 — health-related quality of life in self-management studies

Safer wording:

> “Research has reported improvements in quality of life or psychological well-being in some studied populations.”

Avoid:

> “Massage improves overall health and well-being.”

---

## “Long-term self-care and recovery routine”

**Not directly supported as currently written.**

The Nemati review is useful because it shows massage has been studied as an ongoing self-management strategy, including programs lasting up to 8–12 weeks.

However, those studies primarily involved **self-administered massage**, not ordinary therapist-delivered recurring massage.

Safer wording:

> “Massage has also been studied as part of ongoing self-management for musculoskeletal symptoms.”

If the commercial page needs the concept of a regular wellness practice rather than a medical claim, make it clearly **practice guidance/opinion**, not “research proves.”

# 14. Agent implementation rule

For each website sentence:

```text
EXACT WEBSITE CLAIM
        ↓
MATCH INTERVENTION
        ↓
MATCH POPULATION
        ↓
MATCH OUTCOME
        ↓
CHECK EFFECT SIZE / DIRECTION
        ↓
CHECK CERTAINTY / RISK OF BIAS
        ↓
CHECK DURATION
        ↓
CHECK GENERALIZABILITY
        ↓
WRITE CITATION
```

If a paper supports only part of a sentence, **split the sentence or narrow it**.

If the intervention is sports massage, do not rewrite it as therapeutic massage.

If the intervention is self-administered massage, do not rewrite it as therapist-delivered massage.

If the population is healthcare workers, caregivers, postpartum women, cancer patients, or a specific pain population, do not silently generalize to the general public.

If the study only shows short-term effects, do not write “long-term.”

If certainty is low or very low, the website wording should reflect that.

## Final principle

> **Use the scientific paper to constrain the marketing claim — never stretch the scientific paper to justify the marketing claim.**
