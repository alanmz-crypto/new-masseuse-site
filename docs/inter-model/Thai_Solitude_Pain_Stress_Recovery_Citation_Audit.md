# Thai Solitude — Pain, Stress & Recovery Citation Audit

## Claim
> “Treatments to relieve pain and stress and to support recovery.”

## Verdict
**Supported in substance, but slightly overbroad in wording.** Pain has the strongest evidence; stress is supported with context limits; recovery is supported most clearly for exercise-related soreness/fatigue and selected rehabilitation contexts.

## Recommended wording
> “Treatments to help relieve pain and stress and support recovery.”

### More precise alternative
> “Massage treatments to help ease pain, reduce stress, and support physical recovery.”

## Pain evidence

### Crawford et al., 2016 — Pain Medicine
Systematic review/meta-analysis of 67 studies. Massage was associated with reduced pain in chronic musculoskeletal pain populations.

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27165971/

PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4925170/

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/pm/pnw099

Supports: “help relieve pain.” Does not support a guarantee for every client or superiority to all other pain treatments.

### Mak et al., 2024 — JAMA Network Open
Evidence mapping of 129 systematic reviews. No formally graded conclusion reached high certainty; seven moderate-certainty conclusions were beneficial associations with pain.

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39008297/

PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11250267/

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.22259

### Traditional Thai massage — chronic pain systematic review
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25682523/

Supports that traditional Thai massage itself has been studied in chronic pain populations.

### Thai massage — chronic neck pain randomized trial
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34391301/

Reported improvements in pain, disability, pressure-pain threshold, and cervical range of motion versus control.

## Stress evidence

### Massage/stress systematic review and network meta-analysis
A systematic review and network meta-analysis examined 15 randomized trials involving 688 healthcare workers. Massage was associated with lower occupational stress versus no intervention; reported effect approximately SMD −0.43 (95% CI −0.72 to −0.14).

PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8263904/

Supports “help reduce/ease stress,” not a universal guarantee.

### Sharpe et al., 2007
Randomized study of 54 community-dwelling adults aged 60+ comparing massage with guided relaxation; repeated massage improved perceived stress and several well-being outcomes.

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17709060/

### Physiological stress review
Review of 25 therapist-delivered massage studies found evidence of acute reductions in some stress-related measures, including cortisol and heart rate, but insufficient evidence for strong claims about sustained physiological effects from repeated massage.

PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2892349/

### Limiting evidence
A randomized trial in stressed primary-care patients found all groups improved, but massage did not outperform relaxation tape.

PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1314487/

Therefore “help reduce/ease stress” is preferable to an unqualified “relieve stress.”

## Recovery evidence

The evidence is strongest when “recovery” means recovery from exercise-related muscle soreness/fatigue or selected rehabilitation contexts. It does not establish massage as a general-purpose treatment that accelerates recovery from every injury, illness, or medical condition.

### Dupuy et al., 2018 — post-exercise recovery
Systematic review/meta-analysis of 99 studies. Massage reduced delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS).

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29755363/

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2017-0618

### Davis et al., 2020 — BMJ Open Sport & Exercise Medicine
Systematic review/meta-analysis of 29 randomized studies involving 1,012 participants. Small improvements in flexibility and DOMS; no evidence for improved strength, jumping, sprinting, endurance, or fatigue.

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32426160/

PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7228568/

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjsem-2019-000614

### 2025 umbrella review
Umbrella review of 29 systematic reviews covering 863 unique randomized trials found evidence for massage reducing DOMS at multiple post-exercise time points, while noting methodological limitations and constrained generalizability.

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40120073/

### 2024 postoperative rehabilitation meta-analysis
Systematic review/meta-analysis of 11 randomized trials involving 940 people after total knee arthroplasty found massage improved early postoperative pain and knee range of motion. Evidence quality was low to moderate and heterogeneous.

PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11110294/

### Traditional Thai massage — exercise-fatigue RCT, 2024
Thai massage after experimentally induced gastrocnemius fatigue produced greater improvements in muscle power, EMG-related measures, and perceived fatigue than control.

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38796061/

Supports recovery wording, but only in a specific experimental physical-fatigue context.

## Claim-by-claim audit

| Phrase | Status | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| “relieve pain” | ✅ Supported | Strong peer-reviewed evidence, though effects vary by condition |
| “relieve stress” | ✅ Supported with qualification | Evidence exists but is population/context dependent |
| “support recovery” | ✅ Supported with qualification | Best evidence for post-exercise soreness/fatigue and selected rehabilitation |

## Final recommendation
**KEEP CONCEPT, MODIFY WORDING.**

> **“Treatments to help relieve pain and stress and support recovery.”**

These studies support general statements about massage-related outcomes in studied populations and contexts. They do **not** establish a specific outcome for an individual client or massage as treatment for a particular disease or diagnosis.
