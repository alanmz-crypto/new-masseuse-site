Written 2026-10-19 for Ryan. **Arc: none (ad-hoc).** Question (Ryan): "double check on both of the pages we just researched to make sure I didn't miss any discouraging or poor results ... so we can sandwich the discouraging results between two assertive positive claims", then "you can sandwich with the method you used on the Therapeutic page. ## How I checked

Europe PMC (the PubMed mirror that answers scripts), 2026-10-19: (1) every record with "Thai massage" / "traditional Thai massage" / "Nuad Thai" / "Thai traditional massage" in the title (61 records; 19 were already on the Thai page); (2) every Thai-massage review and every record about harm; (3) for Therapeutic, the reviews and sham- or placebo-controlled trials of massage on pain, movement, soreness, stress and well-being, plus a search for adverse events of massage. I read the abstract of each candidate before using it.

| Missing result | Now on the page |

Already on the page and unchanged in substance: Cochrane neck 2024 (little or no difference vs placebo massage), Cochrane low-back 2015 ("very little confidence"), massage adding nothing to exercise for shoulder pain, no cortisol effect, sham-controlled well-being trial with no significant difference, no evidence for strength, jump, sprint, endurance or fatigue, performance recovery "rather small and partly unclear", oxytocin rising with reading too, caregiver study without a non-massage group.

## The sandwich

Every panel on both pages now runs: **what the studies found in massage's favor, then the weaker, mixed and negative results in full, then an encouraging close**, each sentence sourced and hedged the way its source hedges it. The intro says so ("I included the studies I found with disappointing results, not only the favorable ones") and states the scope. The safety paragraph follows the same shape: generally low risk, rare serious problems reported (named plainly), serious events are rare and mostly tied to unusual techniques or untrained hands, and "I ask about your health ... and I set the pressure ... by what is comfortable for you".

Limits of the method: the positives are as strong as the studies, not stronger (for example "short-term", "small improvements", "a pilot study", "the authors called for larger trials" stay in the sentence), nothing discouraging was shortened to fit the shape, and the audit is a search of one database, not a guarantee that no study exists.

## Who was involved in the injuries (added at Ryan's request)

Ryan asked for the safety paragraph to introduce, and to end on, the point that the injuries came from untrained therapists. What the sources support: the 2014 review (full text read) lists the practitioner for each event: **massage therapists in 8 of 138 events (5.8%)**, chiropractors in 33 (23.9%), and "unregistered or even healthcare professionals only" in the other 97 (70.3%); its authors conclude that "massage by nonprofessional and forceful techniques is often associated with AEs" and caution against lumping well-trained chiropractors' spinal manipulation with the untrained. Ernst 2003: most serious events followed unusual techniques or massage by laymen; massage therapists were rarely implicated. The Thai case report: a layperson, a friend. The 2015 commentary says nothing (in its abstract) about who gave the massage. So both pages now open on "where serious problems on record can be traced to who gave the massage, they are mostly tied to people who were not trained massage therapists", give the breakdown, and close on "trained massage therapists were rarely involved: the serious injuries were mostly linked to people who were not massage therapists, including laypeople and unregistered practitioners, and to forceful or unusual techniques". I did NOT write that "the untrained caused the injuries" without qualification: about a quarter of the events involved chiropractors (trained professionals doing spinal manipulation), the reports are case reports (association, not rates), and the page must not claim more than the review does.
