# Thai Solitude — Final Focused Audit
## Long-Term Self-Care Routine & Deep Tissue A6

**Date:** 2026-10-04

## Purpose

This is the final focused evidence audit of the two remaining weak medical/research claims on the Thai Solitude website:

1. **“Long-term self-care routine”**
2. **A6 — “effective option for many types”**

The standard remains strict:

- peer-reviewed scientific evidence only;
- preferably systematic reviews, meta-analyses, or randomized controlled trials;
- exact intervention and outcome checked;
- population and duration checked;
- website wording must not exceed the evidence;
- therapist-delivered massage must not be conflated with self-administered massage.

---

# 1. Claim: “Long-term self-care routine”

## Current website wording

> “Regular massage has also been associated with improvements in overall well-being, making it a valuable part of a long-term self-care and recovery routine.”

## Final verdict

**The exact sentence remains unsupported as a research claim.**

However, the final search produced an important correction to the earlier assessment:

> **Long-term / recurring therapist-delivered massage absolutely has been studied.**

The evidence does **not** support the broader conclusion that recurring massage is therefore a “valuable part of a long-term self-care and recovery routine” for the general public.

---

# 2. Strong direct evidence: therapist-delivered maintenance massage

## Perlman et al. — randomized clinical trial

**Efficacy and Safety of Massage for Osteoarthritis of the Knee: a Randomized Clinical Trial**

Peer-reviewed randomized clinical trial in *Journal of General Internal Medicine*.

- Publisher: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11606-018-4763-5
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-018-4763-5
- Trial registration: NCT01537484

## Study design

- 222 adults with knee osteoarthritis enrolled
- 60-minute whole-body massage
- weekly massage for 8 weeks
- subsequent biweekly maintenance massage in randomized participants
- assessments through **52 weeks**
- therapist-delivered intervention

This is important because it is **not self-massage**.

## Results

At 8 weeks, massage significantly improved:

- overall WOMAC score;
- pain;
- stiffness;
- physical function.

At 52 weeks:

- the overall between-group difference was no longer statistically significant;
- maintenance massage did **not** significantly improve WOMAC global score, pain, stiffness, ROM, walking time, or pain interference beyond the initial 8-week treatment.

## What this proves

It proves that:

> **Therapist-delivered massage has been studied as a recurring/maintenance treatment over a 52-week period.**

## What it does not prove

It does not prove:

> “Regular massage is a valuable long-term self-care and recovery routine.”

The study was in adults with **knee osteoarthritis**, not healthy general wellness clients.

It also did not show that year-long maintenance created an additional general wellness benefit.

---

# 3. Strong direct evidence: recurring therapist-delivered massage for chronic neck pain

## Cook et al. — randomized clinical trial

**Randomized clinical trial assessing whether additional massage treatments for chronic neck pain improve 12- and 26-week outcomes**

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26096474/
- PMC full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4596391/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.spinee.2015.06.049

## Study design

179 adults with chronic nonspecific neck pain.

Initial treatment:

- 4 weeks of therapist-delivered massage.

Booster phase:

- an additional six weekly 60-minute massage treatments;
- compared with no additional massage.

Follow-up:

- 12 weeks;
- 26 weeks.

## Results

At 12 weeks, booster massage produced:

- significant improvement in neck dysfunction;
- a favorable but not conventionally statistically significant result for pain.

At 26 weeks, the booster effects were no longer statistically significant.

## What this proves

It provides direct evidence that:

> **Repeated therapist-delivered massage and additional “booster” sessions have been scientifically studied as an ongoing treatment strategy.**

## What it does not prove

It does not prove a general long-term wellness/self-care benefit.

It also demonstrates that benefits can diminish after treatment ends.

---

# 4. Very recent evidence: 12 weeks of therapist-delivered massage

## Munk et al. (2026)

**Therapist delivered massage for Veterans with chronic neck pain: a randomized control trial**

- Publisher: https://academic.oup.com/painmedicine/article-abstract/27/2/160/8242775
- DOI / paper identifier: available from the publisher page

## Study design

Veterans with chronic neck pain received:

- twice-weekly;
- 60-minute;
- therapist-delivered;
- standardized massage;
- for 12 weeks.

## Why this matters

This is another modern example of a substantial course of **therapist-delivered recurring massage**, rather than self-massage.

The paper reports outcomes through six months.

The authors discuss diminishing effects after treatment and suggest that longer treatment windows or booster sessions may be worth studying.

## What it supports

It strengthens the factual statement:

> **“Regular/ongoing therapist-delivered massage has been studied.”**

It does not establish:

> **“Regular massage is a valuable long-term self-care routine for the general public.”**

---

# 5. Why “self-care routine” still fails as a scientific conclusion

The key distinction is:

### Evidence-supported

> Massage can be delivered repeatedly over weeks or months.

### Evidence-supported in selected conditions

> Repeated massage can produce symptom/function benefits during or shortly after a treatment course.

### Not established

> Therefore, regular massage should be considered a valuable long-term self-care and recovery routine for general consumers.

That last statement is partly a **practice philosophy / lifestyle recommendation**, not a direct empirical finding.

The scientific studies are condition-specific, protocol-specific, and outcome-specific.

---

# 6. Best final wording options

## Option A — keep it as research

> **“Regular massage has been studied as an ongoing treatment approach, with effects varying by condition, treatment schedule, and duration.”**

### Evidence

- Perlman et al. — 52-week maintenance trial
- Cook et al. — repeated/booster treatment trial
- Munk et al. — 12-week therapist-delivered RCT

This is scientifically defensible.

---

## Option B — consumer-friendly non-research wording

> **“Regular massage can be part of an ongoing wellness routine, depending on your needs and preferences.”**

This is better framed as a service philosophy/practice recommendation rather than an evidence claim.

### Important

Do **not** prepend:

> “Research shows…”

because the research does not establish that general proposition.

---

## Option C — remove the idea entirely

This is the safest option if the goal is a highly conservative medical-claims page.

---

# 7. Final judgment on “long-term self-care”

| Question | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Has recurring therapist-delivered massage been studied? | **Yes** |
| Has maintenance massage been studied over 52 weeks? | **Yes** |
| Has additional/booster therapist massage been studied? | **Yes** |
| Are there studies in which benefits persist beyond the treatment period? | **Yes, in selected conditions and time windows** |
| Does that establish general long-term wellness benefit for the public? | **No** |
| Does it establish that massage is a “valuable” self-care routine? | **No** |
| Can the website accurately say regular massage has been studied as an ongoing treatment? | **Yes** |

## Final disposition

**The original phrase should not remain as a research conclusion.**

But the earlier statement that therapist-delivered long-term/maintenance evidence did not exist is **corrected**.

The true dead end is narrower:

> **“valuable part of a long-term self-care and recovery routine” is not a directly established scientific conclusion.**

---

# 8. Claim A6 — “effective option for many types”

## Current website wording

> “Current research suggests deep tissue massage can be an effective option for many types of muscular discomfort and movement restrictions, though results depend on the underlying cause.”

## Final verdict

**The exact wording still cannot be verified.**

The problem is not lack of positive evidence.

The problem is the breadth of:

> **“many types”**

and the confidence of:

> **“can be an effective option.”**

The deep-tissue evidence is too heterogeneous and condition-specific to support that general statement.

---

# 9. Strong direct deep-tissue evidence

## Bingölbali et al. (2024)

**The effectiveness of deep tissue massage on pain, trigger point, disability, range of motion and quality of life in individuals with myofascial pain syndrome**

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36645811/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/08990220.2023.2165054

### Study

80 participants with myofascial pain syndrome.

Deep tissue massage was added to conventional rehabilitation.

### Outcomes

Improvement was found in:

- pain;
- disability;
- quality of life;
- several neck range-of-motion measures.

### What it supports

Deep tissue massage may benefit pain and movement-related outcomes in a **specific musculoskeletal population**.

### What it does not support

It does not prove effectiveness for:

- all muscular discomfort;
- all movement restrictions;
- all body regions;
- all causes of pain;
- the general population.

---

# 10. Large deep-tissue trial: STONE

## Skillgate et al. (2020)

**Effectiveness of deep tissue massage therapy, and supervised strengthening and stretching exercises for subacute or persistent disabling neck pain**

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31655314/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.msksp.2019.102070

## Study

619 adults with subacute or persistent disabling neck pain.

## Findings

There were short-term benefits in some pain/recovery outcomes.

At 12 months:

- none of the treatment approaches was more effective than advice for pain intensity;
- long-term superiority was not established.

## Significance for A6

This reinforces:

> **“some conditions / some outcomes”**

rather than:

> **“many types of muscular discomfort.”**

---

# 11. Deep-tissue evidence-definition problem

## Koren & Kalichman (2018)

**Deep tissue massage: What are we talking about?**

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29861215/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbmt.2017.05.006

The review is important because:

- there is no universally accepted definition of deep tissue massage;
- protocols differ;
- techniques differ;
- research interventions are heterogeneous;
- this makes broad conclusions difficult.

It reports that favorable outcomes may occur in pain populations and people with decreased range of motion.

### Consequence

A6's “many types” wording is precisely where the literature becomes too heterogeneous to justify a broad generalization.

---

# 12. Broad massage evidence also argues against A6 as written

## Bervoets et al. (2015)

**Massage therapy has short-term benefits for people with common musculoskeletal disorders compared to no treatment: a systematic review**

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26093806/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jphys.2015.05.018

26 randomized trials; 2,565 participants.

### Findings

Short-term pain reduction versus no treatment was found for:

- shoulder pain;
- knee osteoarthritis.

Not for:

- low-back pain;
- neck pain.

Some functional outcomes improved in selected conditions.

### Important interpretation

This does **not** mean massage “doesn't work” for back or neck pain.

It means this review did not find statistically significant short-term pain reduction versus no treatment for those conditions.

That is an important constraint on broad statements.

---

# 13. Recent overall evidence-quality source

## Mak et al. (2024)

**Use of Massage Therapy for Pain, 2018-2023: A Systematic Review**

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39008297/
- PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11250267/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.22259

### Key finding

129 systematic reviews of massage for painful adult health conditions were mapped.

Among reviews that formally rated certainty:

- none reached high certainty;
- seven conclusions reached moderate certainty;
- most other conclusions were lower certainty.

### Why this matters for A6

The evidence base is substantial, but it does not justify strong general efficacy language across “many types” of discomfort.

---

# 14. Can A6 be rescued?

Yes — but only by changing the breadth.

## Best conservative version

> **“Evidence suggests that deep tissue massage may be helpful for pain and movement-related problems in some musculoskeletal conditions, although results vary by condition and individual.”**

### Evidence

- Koren & Kalichman
- Bingölbali et al.
- Skillgate et al.
- Mak et al.

---

## More research-forward version

> **“Randomized trials have reported improvements in pain, range of motion, disability, or related outcomes with deep tissue massage in some specific musculoskeletal populations, but the evidence is heterogeneous and cannot be generalized across all conditions.”**

This is the most scientifically defensible version.

---

# 15. What should NOT be done

Do not keep:

> “effective option for many types of muscular discomfort”

and simply add several citations.

That would make the citations look stronger than they actually are.

Do not convert:

- myofascial pain evidence → all muscular discomfort;
- neck pain evidence → all movement restrictions;
- athletic recovery evidence → general therapeutic massage;
- one DTM protocol → all “deep tissue massage.”

---

# 16. Final two-claim disposition

| Claim | Exact wording fully verified? | Stronger evidence discovered? | Final action |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| **“valuable part of a long-term self-care and recovery routine”** | **No** | **Yes** — therapist-delivered maintenance massage is directly studied | Rewrite as ongoing treatment research or make it clearly non-research practice guidance |
| **A6 “effective option for many types”** | **No** | **Yes** — multiple condition-specific DTM trials/reviews | Replace with “may be helpful… in some musculoskeletal conditions” |

# 17. The scientifically strongest final wording

## Long-term / recurring massage

> **“Regular massage has been studied as an ongoing treatment approach, with effects varying by condition, treatment schedule, and duration.”**

## Deep tissue

> **“Evidence suggests that deep tissue massage may be helpful for pain and movement-related problems in some musculoskeletal conditions, although results vary by condition and individual.”**

These two sentences are much closer to the actual literature than the current copy.

# 19. Final principle

The research does **not** say:

> “Massage is a proven long-term wellness routine.”

It does say:

> **Recurring therapist-delivered massage has been studied, including maintenance schedules lasting many months.**

And the research does **not** say:

> “Deep tissue is effective for many types of muscular discomfort.”

It does say:

> **Specific randomized trials report benefits for specific musculoskeletal populations and outcomes, while the broader deep-tissue evidence remains heterogeneous.**

Those are the claims the website can safely build outward from.
