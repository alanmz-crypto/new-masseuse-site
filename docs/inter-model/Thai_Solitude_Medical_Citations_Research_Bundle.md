# Thai Solitude — Medical Claims Citation Research Bundle

**Prepared:** 2026-10-04  
**Purpose:** Agent-ready source pack for medically/health-related claims on the Thai Solitude website.  
**Scope:** Research evidence only. Excludes personal credentials, licensing, business facts, client-history claims, and Oregon compliance rules.

---

## 1. How to use this file

This file is designed for an agent to:

1. Download/open the primary sources from the URLs below.
2. Match each website claim to the **exact** evidence supported by the source.
3. Prefer systematic reviews/meta-analyses over individual trials when both are available.
4. Keep wording no stronger than the evidence.
5. Do not use a citation to rescue a practitioner-specific outcome promise.
6. If a claim cannot be supported at its current specificity, rewrite the claim rather than merely adding a citation.

**Primary source hierarchy used here:**

1. PubMed-indexed systematic review / meta-analysis
2. PubMed-indexed randomized controlled trial
3. PubMed-indexed narrative/systematic review specific to the modality
4. High-quality evidence map / umbrella-style synthesis for uncertainty/context

## 2. Website claim inventory being sourced

The Thai Solitude citation worklist identifies six Group A research claims: three on Therapeutic Massage and three on Deep Tissue. The worklist explicitly says the source must support the **exact wording** and that unsupported wording should be deleted or rewritten.  

Source file: `Thai Solitude - Citation Worklist.md`

### A1 — Therapeutic Massage

> “Research suggests that massage therapy may help reduce pain, improve mobility, support recovery from physical activity, and promote relaxation. Regular massage has also been associated with improvements in overall well-being, making it a valuable part of a long-term self-care and recovery routine.”

**Current evidence judgment:** Partially supportable. Strongest support is for pain and some function/QOL outcomes; exercise-related evidence supports small improvements in flexibility and delayed-onset muscle soreness. “Valuable part of a long-term self-care and recovery routine” is an interpretive recommendation, not a direct systematic-review finding.

### A2 — Therapeutic Massage

The worklist records a second research sentence but does not capture the text.

**Status:** Do not invent or cite until the exact sentence is captured from the page/source text.

### A3 — Therapeutic Massage

The worklist records a third research sentence but does not capture the text.

**Status:** Do not invent or cite until the exact sentence is captured from the page/source text.

### A4 — Deep Tissue Massage

> “Research suggests deep tissue massage may help reduce pain intensity, improve mobility, and support quality of life in people experiencing ongoing musculoskeletal discomfort.”

**Current evidence judgment:** Supportable only with narrower wording. There is a randomized trial in people with myofascial pain syndrome showing improvement in pain, disability, quality of life, and several neck ROM measures when deep tissue massage was added to conventional rehabilitation. Evidence is not sufficient to generalize to all people with ongoing musculoskeletal discomfort.

### A5 — Deep Tissue Massage

> “Studies have also linked deep tissue massage with improvements in flexibility, muscle recovery, and functional movement, though outcomes vary from person to person.”

**Current evidence judgment:** Too broad as a deep-tissue-specific statement. Systematic-review evidence supports small improvements in flexibility and delayed-onset muscle soreness for massage generally, but the evidence does not justify attributing all three outcomes specifically to “deep tissue massage.”

### A6 — Deep Tissue Massage

> “Current research suggests deep tissue massage can be an effective option for many types of muscular discomfort and movement restrictions, though results depend on the underlying cause.”

**Current evidence judgment:** Too broad. The deep-tissue literature is heterogeneous, lacks a common definition/protocol, and does not support a blanket “effective option for many types” formulation.

# 3. Recommended core source set

## Source S1 — Mak et al. (2024), JAMA Network Open

**Type:** Systematic review / evidence map  
**Use:** Overall evidence quality, pain claims, calibration of certainty  
**PubMed PMID:** 39008297  
**DOI:** 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.22259  

### Citation

Mak S, Allen J, Begashaw M, Miake-Lye IM, Beroes-Severin J, De Vries G, Lawson E, Shekelle PG. *Use of Massage Therapy for Pain, 2018-2023: A Systematic Review.* JAMA Network Open. 2024;7(7):e2422259.

### Key evidence

- Identified **129 systematic reviews** of massage therapy for painful adult health conditions.
- 41 reviews formally rated certainty/quality/strength of evidence.
- No conclusions were rated as **high-certainty** evidence.
- Seven conclusions were rated **moderate-certainty**, and those moderate-certainty conclusions were beneficial associations with pain.
- Conclusions that massage was superior to other active therapies were uncommon.

### What it supports

Good support for cautious wording such as:

> “Evidence suggests massage therapy may help reduce pain in some conditions.”

It also supports an explicit evidence caveat when making broad efficacy statements.

### What it does NOT support

It does not support a blanket claim that massage is broadly effective for all conditions, nor that long-term wellness benefits are established with high certainty.

### URLs

## Source S2 — Crawford et al. (2016), Pain Medicine

**Type:** Systematic review + meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials  
**Use:** General massage evidence for pain, function-related outcomes, anxiety, and health-related quality of life  
**PubMed PMID:** 27165971  
**PMCID:** PMC4925170  
**DOI:** 10.1093/pm/pnw099

### Citation

Crawford C, Boyd C, Paat CF, Price A, Xenakis L, Yang E, Zhang W; Evidence for Massage Therapy Working Group. *The Impact of Massage Therapy on Function in Pain Populations—A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials: Part I, Patients Experiencing Pain in the General Population.* Pain Med. 2016;17(7):1353-1375.

### Key evidence

The review included **67 studies** (60 high quality and 7 low quality). Compared with sham, no treatment, and active comparators, massage therapy showed beneficial effects on pain. The review also found benefits for anxiety and health-related quality of life compared with active comparators.

The paper reports post-treatment analyses involving **activity/range of motion**, including musculoskeletal pain populations.

### What it supports

Potential support for carefully worded statements involving:

- pain reduction
- some function/activity outcomes
- anxiety/relaxation-adjacent outcomes
- health-related quality of life

### Important wording constraint

The review’s own conclusion uses cautious language when massage is compared against sham or active comparators. Do not turn this into a universal “massage works” claim.

- PubMed Central full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4925170/

## Source S3 — Davis et al. (2020), BMJ Open Sport & Exercise Medicine

**Type:** Systematic review + meta-analysis of randomized studies  
**Use:** Flexibility and delayed-onset muscle soreness / recovery-related claims  
**PubMed PMID:** 32426160  
**PMCID:** PMC7228568  
**DOI:** 10.1136/bmjsem-2019-000614

### Citation

Davis HL, Alabed S, Chico TJA. *Effect of sports massage on performance and recovery: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* BMJ Open Sport Exerc Med. 2020;6(1):e000614.

### Key evidence

The review identified **29 eligible studies involving 1,012 participants**.

It found:

- no evidence that massage improved strength, jumping, sprinting, endurance, or fatigue;
- **small but statistically significant improvements in flexibility**;
- **small but statistically significant improvements in delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS)**.

### What it supports

This is the strongest source in this bundle for rewriting A5 around:

> “Research on massage has found small improvements in flexibility and exercise-related muscle soreness.”

### What it does NOT support

It does not establish that deep tissue massage specifically improves “functional movement,” and it does not establish that massage broadly improves athletic performance.

## Source S4 — Bingölbali et al. (2024), Somatosensory & Motor Research

**Type:** Randomized controlled trial  
**Use:** Deep-tissue-specific evidence for pain, disability, ROM, and quality of life  
**PubMed PMID:** 36645811  
**DOI:** 10.1080/08990220.2023.2165054

### Citation

Bingölbali Ö, Taşkaya C, Alkan H, Altındağ Ö. *The effectiveness of deep tissue massage on pain, trigger point, disability, range of motion and quality of life in individuals with myofascial pain syndrome.* Somatosensory & Motor Research. 2024;41(1):11-17.

### Key evidence

The trial compared a deep-tissue-massage group with control treatment in individuals with **myofascial pain syndrome**.

Reported results included statistically greater improvement in the deep-tissue group for:

- pain (VAS)
- disability (NPDS)
- quality of life (SF-36)
- several neck range-of-motion measures

Flexion was an exception: there was no significant between-group difference for that measurement.

### What it supports

A4 can be rewritten around a specific population:

> “In a randomized trial involving people with myofascial pain syndrome, adding deep tissue massage to conventional rehabilitation improved pain, disability, quality of life, and several measures of neck range of motion.”

For website copy, this is more specific than the current general claim but much more defensible.

### What it does NOT support

It does not establish that deep tissue massage is effective for every person with “ongoing musculoskeletal discomfort.”

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36645811/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/08990220.2023.2165054

## Source S5 — Koren & Kalichman (2018), Journal of Bodywork & Movement Therapies

**Type:** Peer-reviewed narrative review specifically about deep tissue massage  
**Use:** Evidence limitation / modality definition / avoiding overclaiming  
**PubMed PMID:** 29861215  
**DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbmt.2017.05.006

### Citation

Koren Y, Kalichman L. *Deep tissue massage: What are we talking about?* J Bodyw Mov Ther. 2018;22(2):247-251.

### Key evidence

The review reports:

- there is **no commonly accepted definition** of deep tissue massage in the literature;
- published studies use heterogeneous techniques and protocols;
- this heterogeneity makes it difficult to draw clear efficacy conclusions;
- favorable outcomes may occur for pain populations and people with decreased range of motion;
- rare serious adverse events have been reported, probably related to forceful massage application.

### Why this source matters for Thai Solitude

This is the source that most strongly argues **against** A6 as currently written. It is not a “deep tissue works broadly” source. It says the evidence is heterogeneous and that clearer conclusions require standardized definitions and protocols.

### What it supports

A cautious formulation such as:

> “Evidence for deep tissue massage is mixed and the techniques used in research vary, so outcomes cannot be generalized across all conditions.”

### URLs

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29861215/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbmt.2017.05.006

# 8. Claims that should NOT be “fixed” by adding citations

Do not use these research sources to justify practitioner-specific or unsupported outcome claims, including:

- “Most clients notice…”
- “Clients regularly report…”
- “You will feel…”
- “The result is more lasting…”
- “Can reduce headache frequency and intensity over time” when presented as a promised client outcome
- claims that the practitioner serves oncology, neurological, disability, or other clinical populations merely because research exists for those populations

These belong to the separate Group B/compliance pass, not this medical citation set.

# 11. Agent instructions for citation insertion

When editing the website:

1. Preserve the citation’s **scope**. Do not broaden a study from a specific condition/population to all massage clients.
2. Prefer a citation immediately after the sentence or paragraph it supports.
3. Name the study/author or institution on the page if the site's citation format allows it.
4. Use PubMed or DOI links as the stable reader-verifiable reference.
5. Do not cite the website's own claims as evidence.
6. If multiple sources support one carefully scoped sentence, use the smallest set that completely supports it.
7. If no source supports the exact sentence, rewrite the sentence before adding a citation.
8. Do not use S4 to support claims about all deep-tissue clients; it is a specific randomized trial in people with myofascial pain syndrome.
9. Use S5 as a **limitation source** as well as an evidence source: it documents heterogeneity and lack of a standard definition for deep tissue massage.
10. For broad massage-for-pain claims, S1 should be consulted before finalizing wording because it is the newest broad evidence synthesis in this bundle.
