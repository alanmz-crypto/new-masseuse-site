<div class="wp-block-uagb-advanced-heading uagb-block-1215b516"><h2 class="uagb-heading-text">What Is Thai Massage?</h2><p class="uagb-desc-text">Thai massage brings movement and massage together through rhythmic compression, gentle rocking, and assisted stretching. It is my specialty. I set the pace and range of each movement by what is comfortable for you, so you have time to slow down while stiff areas get careful attention.<br><br>As the session goes on, I apply steady pressure and guide you through stretches that can help you notice where your body feels restricted. Tell me what feels good, ask for a lighter touch, or skip a movement at any point. I want you to feel supported the whole way through.<br><br>Thai massage suits people who enjoy hands-on bodywork but also want gentle movement. You stay fully clothed, so wear something loose and comfortable. I shape each session around the person in front of me, so the work can be restful, more active, or a blend of both.<br><br>Traditional Thai massage traces its roots to everyday self-care in Thai villages, and in 2019 UNESCO added Nuad Thai, traditional Thai massage, to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.<a href="https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/nuad-thai-traditional-thai-massage-01384">[1]</a> I trained in traditional Thai bodywork at Still Light Center.</p></div>

<div class="wp-block-uagb-advanced-heading uagb-block-7e4a91c2"><div class="uagb-separator"></div><h2 class="uagb-heading-text">What the Research Says About Thai Massage</h2></div>
<!-- /wp:uagb/advanced-heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Research on traditional Thai massage is still limited. Most studies are small, many compare Thai massage with another approach rather than with no massage, and most follow people for only a few weeks. The summaries below describe what the studies reported. They are general information, not medical advice or a promise of any result, and people respond differently. This section covers studies of massage for pain, movement, stress, energy and recovery; studies of massage used for specific medical conditions are not summarized here. Each part starts with what the studies found in massage’s favor, then gives the results that were weaker, mixed or negative, and ends on what is encouraging: I included the studies I found with disappointing results, not only the favorable ones.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:details {"showContent":true,"style":{"border":{"width":"1px","style":"solid","color":"var(\u002d\u002dts-rule)","radius":"4px"},"color":{"background":"var(\u002d\u002dts-panel)"},"spacing":{"padding":{"top":"0.85rem","right":"1.1rem","bottom":"0.85rem","left":"1.1rem"},"margin":{"bottom":"0.7rem"}},"typography":{"fontWeight":"600"}}} -->
<details class="wp-block-details has-border-color has-background" style="border-color:var(--ts-rule);border-width:1px;border-style:solid;border-radius:4px;background-color:var(--ts-panel);padding-top:0.85rem;padding-right:1.1rem;padding-bottom:0.85rem;padding-left:1.1rem;margin-bottom:0.7rem;font-weight:600" open><summary>Reviews, and What They Say About Limits</summary>
<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">A 2015 systematic review of six studies of traditional Thai massage in people with long-lasting pain found that every study reported less pain after a course of sessions, with the reduction appearing to last for up to 15 weeks.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25682523/">[2]</a> A 2012 review of six studies of Swedish and Thai massage for low-back pain reported that the two appeared to be about equally helpful.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23059437/">[3]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">The reviews also say what they could not show. The 2015 review’s authors said more research is needed to learn how Thai massage compares with control conditions over the longer term, and the 2012 review noted that weaknesses in the studies mean more direct comparisons are needed. Reviews of massage in general are cautious, too. A 2015 Cochrane review of 25 trials with 3,096 people with low-back pain found some short-term benefits, but the studies were small and of low quality, and its authors said they had “very little confidence” in the results.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26329399/">[4]</a> A 2024 Cochrane review of 33 studies with 1,994 people with neck pain found that massage probably made little or no difference compared with a placebo massage after about 12 weeks (low-certainty evidence).<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38415786/">[5]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">That neck review also found that higher doses, meaning eight or more sessions of at least 30 minutes over four weeks, favored massage in a subgroup analysis, which suggests that regular sessions may matter.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38415786/">[5]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">Massage is generally described as low risk, and where serious problems on record can be traced to who gave the massage, they are mostly tied to people who were not trained massage therapists. The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health notes that the risk of harm appears to be low, although rare serious side effects such as a blood clot, nerve injury or bone fracture have been reported.<a href="https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/massage-therapy-what-you-need-to-know">[6]</a> A 2014 review of 40 published reports found 138 adverse events over 11 years, mainly disc herniation, soft-tissue injury, nerve problems, spinal cord injury and tears in the neck arteries, with spinal manipulation inside a massage repeatedly linked to the serious ones. Massage therapists were the practitioners in only 8 of the 138 events (5.8 percent) and chiropractors in 33 (23.9 percent); the rest (70.3 percent) involved unregistered people or other health professionals, and the authors concluded that massage by nonprofessionals and forceful techniques is often associated with adverse events.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25197310/">[7]</a> A 2003 review of 16 case reports and four case series likewise found that most adverse effects followed exotic techniques or massage by laypeople, with massage therapists rarely implicated, that serious effects were mostly tied to techniques other than Swedish massage, and concluded that serious events are probably true rarities.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12777645/">[8]</a> For Thai massage specifically, a 2019 case report described a 20-year-old man who developed bleeding around the spinal cord (a spinal epidural hematoma) with delayed leg weakness three days after a Thai massage given by a layperson, a friend, and who needed emergency surgery.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30691861/">[9]</a> A 2015 commentary also discussed a patient who had a heart attack around the time of a Thai massage and the possible adverse effects of Thai massage.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26865746/">[10]</a> The U.S. center also advises against using massage to put off seeing a health care provider about a medical problem. Taken together, in the reports that say who gave the massage, trained massage therapists were rarely involved: the serious injuries were mostly linked to people who were not massage therapists, including laypeople and unregistered practitioners, and to forceful or unusual techniques. Serious events are rare: I ask about your health, medications and recent injuries before each session, and I set the pressure and the range of each stretch by what is comfortable for you.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
</details>

<details class="wp-block-details has-border-color has-background" style="border-color:var(--ts-rule);border-width:1px;border-style:solid;border-radius:4px;background-color:var(--ts-panel);padding-top:0.85rem;padding-right:1.1rem;padding-bottom:0.85rem;padding-left:1.1rem;margin-bottom:0.7rem;font-weight:600"><summary>Back Pain</summary>
<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">Two trials with a comparison that was not another massage reported clear differences. In 50 adults with upper-back pain, one 30-minute session of Thai massage was followed by larger reductions in pain, muscle tension and muscle activity than a sham device.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27670864/">[11]</a> In 36 adults with back pain, one 30-minute session was followed by lower self-reported pain, anxiety and muscle tension and better flexibility than resting on a bed.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21147414/">[12]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">Trials that compared Thai massage with another approach found similar results rather than a winner. In a hospital trial in Bangkok, 120 adults had Thai massage or joint mobilization twice a week for four weeks, and both groups improved by similar amounts on pain and everyday-activity scores. The trial had no comparison group that received neither, so it cannot show how much of the change was due to the sessions themselves.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28690703/">[13]</a> In 140 older adults, Thai massage alone and Thai massage with an herbal compress led to similar improvements over 15 weeks, so the compress added no clear benefit.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31987237/">[14]</a> In another trial of 140 adults, adding acupuncture to court-type Thai massage made no clear difference after one month, and both groups reported less pain and more movement.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42142427/">[15]</a> A preliminary trial of 18 military personnel with back pain found less pain after both Thai massage and acupuncture, with one pain-questionnaire score lower after acupuncture.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21299184/">[16]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">An earlier trial that compared Thai and Swedish massage for back pain with tender spots in the muscles found that pain fell by more than half in both groups after three weeks and stayed lower for up to a month, with no significant difference between them.<a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbmt.2005.02.001">[17]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
</details>
<!-- /wp:details -->

<!-- wp:details {"style":{"border":{"width":"1px","style":"solid","color":"var(\u002d\u002dts-rule)","radius":"4px"},"color":{"background":"var(\u002d\u002dts-panel)"},"spacing":{"padding":{"top":"0.85rem","right":"1.1rem","bottom":"0.85rem","left":"1.1rem"},"margin":{"bottom":"0.7rem"}},"typography":{"fontWeight":"600"}}} -->
<details class="wp-block-details has-border-color has-background" style="border-color:var(--ts-rule);border-width:1px;border-style:solid;border-radius:4px;background-color:var(--ts-panel);padding-top:0.85rem;padding-right:1.1rem;padding-bottom:0.85rem;padding-left:1.1rem;margin-bottom:0.7rem;font-weight:600"><summary>Neck, Shoulders, Arms and Feet</summary>
<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">In 45 adults with neck pain, eight sessions of Thai massage or of a muscle energy technique over two weeks were each followed by more improvement in pain and neck movement than in a control group.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34391301/">[18]</a> In 78 adults with shoulder-blade pain, four weeks of Thai massage or Thai boxing exercise both reduced pain, and Thai massage did better on several measures, including pain and neck movement, at the end of the program and at one month.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35576853/">[19]</a> In 46 adults with upper-shoulder pain, court-type Thai massage and a Thai hermit exercise routine both improved neck movement and pain, and the massage group reported a clearer reduction in pain.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34293959/">[20]</a> In 62 young adults with tender spots in the forearm muscles, nine short sessions of Thai massage were followed by greater improvement in pain, tenderness and wrist movement than sham ultrasound.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39593435/">[21]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">Not every comparison favored Thai massage. In the neck trial there was no clear difference between Thai massage and the muscle energy technique. In 60 people with heel pain, both Thai massage and ultrasound were followed by less pain, but ultrasound with stretching produced larger gains in pain and ankle movement.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39905464/">[22]</a> In 48 adults with a forward head posture, a four-week program that combined Thai massage with shoulder-blade exercises and chest mobilization improved head position and neck movement more than lying at rest. Because three approaches were combined, the trial cannot show what Thai massage added by itself.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37605282/">[23]</a> In 30 healthy female office workers who each tried three styles of Thai massage, the authors reported that “strength in shoulders was not significantly different, but some forearm fatigue was decreased significantly among the three massage techniques.”<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25892379/">[24]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">A pilot trial of 20 people with shoulder-blade pain found lower pain and muscle tension after Thai massage than after a physical-therapy package of ultrasound and a hot pack.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22196428/">[25]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
</details>
<!-- /wp:details -->

<!-- wp:details {"style":{"border":{"width":"1px","style":"solid","color":"var(\u002d\u002dts-rule)","radius":"4px"},"color":{"background":"var(\u002d\u002dts-panel)"},"spacing":{"padding":{"top":"0.85rem","right":"1.1rem","bottom":"0.85rem","left":"1.1rem"},"margin":{"bottom":"0.7rem"}},"typography":{"fontWeight":"600"}}} -->
<details class="wp-block-details has-border-color has-background" style="border-color:var(--ts-rule);border-width:1px;border-style:solid;border-radius:4px;background-color:var(--ts-panel);padding-top:0.85rem;padding-right:1.1rem;padding-bottom:0.85rem;padding-left:1.1rem;margin-bottom:0.7rem;font-weight:600"><summary>Stress, Energy and Recovery</summary>
<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">In 54 healthy adults whose calf muscles were tired by heel-raise exercise, 30 minutes of Thai massage was followed by faster recovery on muscle measures than 30 minutes of mostly rest.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38796061/">[26]</a> In 34 soccer players who were given Thai massage or rest, all seven fitness tests (sit-and-reach, hand grip strength, agility, a 50-meter sprint, sit-ups, push-ups and a test of aerobic fitness) improved in the massage group, compared with two in the rest group.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25729203/">[27]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">The results on stress are mixed. Two small trials in healthy adults first raised stress with a mental-arithmetic test and then gave either one hour of Thai massage or one hour of rest. In both trials, stress measures fell after massage and after rest. In one trial (29 people, each trying both), one marker, salivary amylase, fell more after Thai massage; in the other (30 people), no measure differed between the groups.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26436433/">[28]</a><a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26387408/">[29]</a> In 36 physiotherapy students under academic stress, saliva cortisol and heart rate fell after one Thai massage session and in a control group, with no significant difference in cortisol between the groups.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27210845/">[30]</a> In a small crossover trial, 20 adults who felt tired or low on energy had three weekly sessions of Thai massage and three of Swedish massage, and Swedish massage was described as having the larger effect on relaxation and sleep.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30100318/">[31]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">Even so, the results point the same encouraging way on the outcomes that matter to many people: a marker of stress, salivary amylase, fell more after Thai massage than after rest in one trial,<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26436433/">[28]</a> and in the trial of tired adults Thai massage was described as the more energizing of the two styles.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30100318/">[31]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
</details>
