<div class="wp-block-uagb-advanced-heading uagb-block-d1bf4422"><h2 class="uagb-heading-text">What Is Deep Tissue Massage?</h2><p class="uagb-desc-text">Deep tissue massage uses slow, deliberate pressure on muscles that stay tight. I begin by asking about your goals and how much pressure you like, then check in as I work. Deep pressure is never required: you can ask me to lighten up at any time, and I will.<br><br>Research on deep tissue massage is limited and mixed. In a trial of 80 adults with neck pain linked to tender spots in the muscles, adding deep tissue massage to conventional rehabilitation was followed by more improvement in pain, quality-of-life scores and most neck movements than rehabilitation alone.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36645811/">[1]</a> A larger trial of 619 adults with ongoing neck pain found that deep tissue massage led to more improvement in pain than advice to stay active in the short term but not by 12 months, and a review of the research found that studies differ too much in what they call deep tissue massage and how they do it to draw clear conclusions.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31655314/">[2]</a><a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29861215/">[3]</a> People given massage, exercise or both in that larger trial were also more likely than those given advice to say they had recovered, at every follow-up through one year.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31655314/">[2]</a></p></div>

<div class="wp-block-uagb-advanced-heading uagb-block-74d424ce"><div class="uagb-separator"></div><h2 class="uagb-heading-text">What the Research Says About Deep Tissue Massage</h2></div>
<!-- /wp:uagb/advanced-heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Research on deep tissue massage is limited, and the studies are hard to compare: a 2018 review found no commonly accepted definition of deep tissue massage, and the studies used different techniques and protocols.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29861215/">[3]</a> Many studies are small, results often depend on what massage is compared with, and many follow people for only a few weeks. The summaries below describe what the studies reported. They are general information, not medical advice or a promise of any result, and people respond differently. This section covers studies of massage for pain, movement, soreness, recovery and relaxation; studies of massage used for specific medical conditions are not summarized here. Each part starts with what the studies found in massage’s favor, then gives the results that were weaker, mixed or negative, and ends on what is encouraging: I included the studies I found with disappointing results, not only the favorable ones.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:details {"showContent":true,"style":{"border":{"width":"1px","style":"solid","color":"var(\u002d\u002dts-rule)","radius":"4px"},"color":{"background":"var(\u002d\u002dts-panel)"},"spacing":{"padding":{"top":"0.85rem","right":"1.1rem","bottom":"0.85rem","left":"1.1rem"},"margin":{"bottom":"0.7rem"}},"typography":{"fontWeight":"600"}}} -->
<details class="wp-block-details has-border-color has-background" style="border-color:var(--ts-rule);border-width:1px;border-style:solid;border-radius:4px;background-color:var(--ts-panel);padding-top:0.85rem;padding-right:1.1rem;padding-bottom:0.85rem;padding-left:1.1rem;margin-bottom:0.7rem;font-weight:600" open><summary>Reviews, and What They Say About Limits</summary>
<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">Reviews of massage in general, not only deep tissue massage, reached a consistent point in massage’s favor: every conclusion rated moderate-certainty found massage associated with less pain, and a meta-analysis of 67 randomized trials found lower pain scores after massage than after sham massage, no massage or other approaches.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39008297/">[4]</a><a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27165971/">[5]</a> A 2018 review of the research on deep tissue massage itself reported that favorable outcomes may result in people with pain and in people with decreased range of motion.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29861215/">[3]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">The same reviews are careful about limits. None of the conclusions in the 2024 review was rated high-certainty, and conclusions that massage was better than other active approaches were rare.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39008297/">[4]</a> An earlier evidence map of 49 systematic reviews found that the high-quality reviews reached conclusions of low strength of evidence, because few large, rigorous studies had been done.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30892910/">[6]</a> The meta-analysis’s authors made a strong recommendation only for the comparison with no massage and a weak one for sham massage and other approaches.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27165971/">[5]</a> The 2018 review of deep tissue massage found that the techniques and protocols used in published studies differed so much that it was difficult to draw any clear conclusions, and its authors asked for future research to use a common definition and common comparison groups.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29861215/">[3]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">Massage is generally described as low risk, but deep pressure deserves a plain account of the rare serious events on record. The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health notes that the risk of harm appears to be low, although rare serious side effects such as a blood clot, nerve injury or bone fracture have been reported.<a href="https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/massage-therapy-what-you-need-to-know">[7]</a> The 2018 review of deep tissue massage found several rare serious adverse events related to it, probably as a result of the forceful application of massage therapy.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29861215/">[3]</a> Published case reports describe a blocked artery to part of a kidney in a healthy man about four hours after a deep tissue massage for low back pain given by an unlicensed therapist,<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41727343/">[8]</a> a blood clot in a neck vein after a deep tissue massage focused on the neck,<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40995160/">[9]</a> a blood clot in a vein after vigorous deep tissue massage in a previously healthy 67-year-old man, whose authors suggested that vigorous massage of the legs be considered a possible risk factor for such clots, especially in older adults,<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21150153/">[10]</a> and an injury to a nerve that left a shoulder muscle weak after deep tissue massage.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19887226/">[11]</a> Single case reports show that something happened, not how often. A 2014 review of 40 published reports of massage found 138 adverse events over 11 years, mainly disc herniation, soft-tissue injury, nerve problems, spinal cord injury and tears in the neck arteries, with spinal manipulation inside a massage repeatedly linked to the serious ones. Massage therapists were the practitioners in only 8 of the 138 events (5.8 percent) and chiropractors in 33 (23.9 percent); the rest (70.3 percent) involved unregistered people or other health professionals, and the authors concluded that massage by nonprofessionals and forceful techniques is often associated with adverse events.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25197310/">[12]</a> A 2003 review of published case reports likewise found that most serious events followed unusual techniques or massage by laypeople rather than by massage therapists, and concluded that serious events are probably true rarities.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12777645/">[13]</a> The U.S. center also advises against using massage to put off seeing a health care provider about a medical problem. Taken together, serious events are rare and, where the reports say who gave the massage, they were mostly linked to people who were not massage therapists and to forceful or unusual techniques. Deep pressure is never required: before your session I ask you to tell me about health conditions, medications, pregnancy and recent injury or surgery, and you can ask for lighter pressure or a pause at any time.</p>

<details class="wp-block-details has-border-color has-background" style="border-color:var(--ts-rule);border-width:1px;border-style:solid;border-radius:4px;background-color:var(--ts-panel);padding-top:0.85rem;padding-right:1.1rem;padding-bottom:0.85rem;padding-left:1.1rem;margin-bottom:0.7rem;font-weight:600"><summary>Neck, Shoulders and Back</summary>
<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">Several trials of deep tissue massage report short-term gains for neck and back pain. In 80 adults with neck pain linked to tender spots in the muscles, one group had 12 sessions of deep tissue massage on top of electrical stimulation, hot packs and ultrasound, and the other had only those three; the massage group improved more on pain, a neck-disability score and a quality-of-life score, and on neck extension, side bending and turning, but not on forward bending.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36645811/">[1]</a> In 64 adults, 60 of whom finished, with chronic low back pain that had no specific cause, tender-point deep tissue massage plus lumbar traction was followed by a higher pressure pain threshold, less muscle hardness and less pain than traction alone: pain scores fell by 1.9 with massage and 1.4 with traction alone.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23427384/">[14]</a> In 26 adults aged 60 to 75 with chronic low back pain, ten days of deep tissue massage and ten days of therapeutic massage both improved pain and disability scores, and deep tissue massage did better on one disability score and on pain.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22744541/">[15]</a> In 40 office workers with chronic low back pain, four 45-minute deep tissue massage sessions over two weeks were followed by better lower-back movement and lower pain ratings in the massage group, while the group given no therapy showed no significant changes.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41270086/">[16]</a> A 2015 Cochrane review of 25 trials found that massage was better than no intervention for low-back pain in the short term.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26329399/">[17]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">The larger and more carefully designed studies are more cautious. In the Stockholm Neck trial of 619 adults with ongoing neck pain, deep tissue massage led to a higher chance of improvement in pain than advice to stay active in the short term but not at later follow-ups, and at 12 months none of the therapies tested was more effective than advice for pain intensity or for pain-related disability.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31655314/">[2]</a> A cost analysis of the same trial found that massage and the combined therapy cost more and were less beneficial than advice, that differences in health-related quality of life were minimal, and its authors concluded that massage and the combined therapy are not cost-effective.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31989965/">[18]</a> The 2024 Cochrane review of massage for neck pain, which included 33 studies with 1,994 people, found that, compared with a placebo massage, massage probably makes little or no difference to pain, function or quality of life at up to 12 weeks (low-certainty evidence), and adverse events were too poorly reported to judge.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38415786/">[19]</a> The 2015 Cochrane review of low-back pain rated the quality of the evidence low to very low, found no benefit over the long term, and its authors said they had “very little confidence” in the results.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26329399/">[17]</a> In 59 adults with chronic low back pain who had two weeks of deep tissue massage with or without a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, pain and function improved in both groups with no difference between them; because everyone had massage, the trial cannot show how much of the change was due to the massage itself.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24707200/">[20]</a> In the office-worker trial, the two groups differed significantly in only one lower-back movement, and the authors said larger studies and an assessment of long-term effects are needed.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41270086/">[16]</a> The small trial of 26 older adults ended by saying that further research is needed.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22744541/">[15]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">Still, people in the Stockholm Neck trial who had massage, exercise or both were more likely than those given advice to say they had recovered, at every follow-up through one year.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31655314/">[2]</a> A subgroup analysis in the 2024 Cochrane review favored higher doses (eight or more sessions of at least 30 minutes over four weeks), which suggests that regular sessions may matter.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38415786/">[19]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<details class="wp-block-details has-border-color has-background" style="border-color:var(--ts-rule);border-width:1px;border-style:solid;border-radius:4px;background-color:var(--ts-panel);padding-top:0.85rem;padding-right:1.1rem;padding-bottom:0.85rem;padding-left:1.1rem;margin-bottom:0.7rem;font-weight:600"><summary>Exercise, Soreness and Recovery</summary>
<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">Deep massage has shown benefit in a laboratory model of muscle soreness: in a double-blinded randomized trial in volunteers whose forearm muscles were made sore with exercise, deep tissue massage reduced pain during a muscle stretch (about a 48 percent reversal of the soreness), and both deep massage and light touch reduced sensitivity to pressure compared with a group that had no massage.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18455480/">[21]</a> A 2020 review of 29 randomized studies with 1,012 people found small improvements in flexibility and in delayed-onset muscle soreness after sports massage.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32426160/">[22]</a> A 2025 trial of 150 athletes who received bi-weekly 40-minute deep tissue massage for 8 weeks reported the greatest performance improvement in team and strength athletes, better recovery outcomes with bi-weekly sessions, and more flexibility with longer sessions.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40510040/">[23]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">Several studies found little or nothing. In the soreness trial, resting pain did not differ between the groups.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18455480/">[21]</a> In 29 healthy volunteers, ten minutes of deep massage to the calf produced no change in calf muscle stiffness or ankle movement, and the authors concluded that using massage to increase tissue flexibility before activity is not justified.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26396740/">[24]</a> In 9 people who did a hard cycling effort, deep massage, superficial massage and stretching did not change blood lactate levels compared with passive rest, and the authors concluded that the pressure used did not play a significant role.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23256711/">[25]</a> In 19 competitive swimmers aged 12 to 20, deep massage and superficial (light-touch) massage between training sessions had no effect on sprint or functional performance, and the swimmers reported worse feelings of heaviness and pain at the main stages of swim training although they also reported less tiredness; the authors suggested the swimmers were able to train harder with no harm to recovery.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36767051/">[26]</a> The 2020 review found no evidence that massage improved strength, jumping, sprinting, endurance or fatigue.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32426160/">[22]</a> A 2016 review of 22 trials concluded that the effects of massage on performance recovery are rather small and partly unclear, and its authors questioned whether the limited effects justify using massage as a recovery method in competitive athletes.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26744335/">[27]</a> In people with sore leg muscles after exercise, manual massage, real diathermy (a heating device) and sham diathermy produced no clinically relevant differences in pain scores right after the session, although scores on a patient-rated scale of global change did differ between the groups.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31956433/">[28]</a> The 2025 athlete trial’s abstract describes no comparison group, and it measured outcomes with both objective and subjective assessments.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40510040/">[23]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">Where massage has been tested for short-term recovery after intense mixed training, the effects were medium in size: the 2016 review found medium positive effects after high-intensity mixed exercise, and shorter massage (5 to 12 minutes) tended to show larger effects than longer massage.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26744335/">[27]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
</details>

<details class="wp-block-details has-border-color has-background" style="border-color:var(--ts-rule);border-width:1px;border-style:solid;border-radius:4px;background-color:var(--ts-panel);padding-top:0.85rem;padding-right:1.1rem;padding-bottom:0.85rem;padding-left:1.1rem;margin-bottom:0.7rem;font-weight:600"><summary>Relaxation and the Role of Pressure</summary>
<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">Several studies point to short-term relaxation after massage. A 2004 meta-analysis of 37 randomized studies found that a single massage was followed by lower state anxiety, blood pressure and heart rate.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14717648/">[29]</a> In 263 volunteers with moderate or severe muscle spasm or strain who had a 45 to 60 minute deep tissue massage, average heart rate was 10.8 beats per minute lower and diastolic blood pressure 5.3 mm Hg lower afterward.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18315516/">[30]</a> In 20 healthy adults, a reflex response in the calf muscle was weaker during massage than at rest, and weaker still during deep massage than during light massage; the study measured a laboratory reflex, not pain or movement.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1589464/">[31]</a> In 60 healthy women, the two massage groups showed higher heart-rate variability than a resting group.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32901072/">[32]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">The picture is mixed, though. The same 2004 meta-analysis found no lower negative mood, cortisol or less immediate pain after a single massage.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14717648/">[29]</a> In the 263-volunteer study, the lower systolic and mean arterial pressure did not clearly reach statistical significance, the abstract describes no comparison group, and its authors said additional research is warranted.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18315516/">[30]</a> In the 60 healthy women, relaxation rose and stress fell in the resting group too.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32901072/">[32]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"style":{"typography":{"fontWeight":"400"}}} -->
<p style="font-weight:400">Courses of massage have the more encouraging results: the largest effects in the 2004 meta-analysis came after a course of sessions rather than a single one, on measures of anxiety and mood.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14717648/">[29]</a> In a pilot study of 54 adults aged 60 and over, twice-weekly massage for four weeks was followed by better scores for well-being and perceived stress than guided relaxation.<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17709060/">[33]</a></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
</details>
